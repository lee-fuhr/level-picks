Spirit Level questions answered

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

This guide answers the most common questions about spirit levels, from types and accuracy to care and selection. Whether you're hanging shelves or framing a wall, understanding these fundamentals will help you choose and use a level with confidence.

What are the different types of spirit levels and which one should I choose?

The main types are box levels, I-beam levels, torpedo levels, and digital levels. Box levels are rigid and durable, ideal for heavy construction. I-beam levels are lighter and often used for general carpentry and hanging cabinets. Torpedo levels are compact, fitting in tight spots like inside cabinets or around pipes. Digital levels give a precise angle readout and can be easier to read in low light. Your choice depends on the job. For everyday DIY around the home a medium-length I-beam or box level is versatile. For trim work and shelves a shorter torpedo level works well. For long spans like countertops or leveling floors a 4-foot level is best. Consider magnetic versions if you work near metal studs or ductwork; just be sure the magnets have an on/off switch so they don’t affect readings on steel beams.

How do I check if my spirit level is accurate?

The classic test is simple. Place the level on a flat, level surface, a countertop or a clean floor. Note where the bubble sits. Then flip the level end-for-end and place it on the exact same spot. If the bubble lands in the same position, the vial is accurate. For the plumb (vertical) vial, hold the level against a wall or door jamb that you know is vertical (or at least vertical enough), note the bubble, then rotate the level 180 degrees and check again. If the bubble shifts between these two positions, the level needs adjusting. Many quality levels have a small screw or adjustment mechanism. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions, or return it if it’s new. A level that fails this test will give you false readings, so it’s worth checking any new level out of the box.

What does the accuracy rating mean, like 0.5 mm/m?

Accuracy ratings tell you how much error is acceptable per unit of length. A common rating is 0.5 mm per meter (or 0.029 degrees). This means over a one-meter length, the level can be off by up to half a millimeter and still be considered within spec. Better levels claim 0.3 mm/m or even 0.15 mm/m for precision work. For framing and rough construction, a rating of 0.5 mm/m is plenty. For finish carpentry, tile setting, or installing countertops, look for a level rated 0.3 mm/m or tighter. Digital levels often advertise accuracy to 0.1 or 0.2 degrees. Remember that accuracy is only meaningful if the level is also properly calibrated, any level can drift if dropped.

Can a spirit level be used outdoors in cold weather?

Yes, but the liquid inside the vial can freeze if temperatures drop well below freezing. Standard spirit levels typically use a colored alcohol or hydrocarbon mixture that remains liquid down to about -20°F or so. If you work in extreme cold, look for levels specifically marked as low-temperature or cold-weather rated. Another cold-weather concern: plastic bodies can become brittle and crack. Many quality levels use a shock-absorbing material like polyurethane or a reinforced frame. If you leave a level in an unheated truck overnight in subzero temps, let it warm up inside before use, thermal expansion can shift the bubble temporarily.

How do I clean and store my spirit level to keep it accurate?

Keep the vial clean and the frame free of dirt and paint. For routine cleaning, wipe with a damp cloth and mild soap; avoid harsh solvents like acetone that can attack the vial seals or the plastic face. If the level gets wet, dry it thoroughly before storing to prevent rust on the frame (especially with box levels that have steel edges). Store your level flat or hanging on a pegboard. Never toss it into a toolbox where tools can dent or bend the frame. A bent frame will ruin accuracy regardless of how good the vial is. Some levels come with a protective case, use it. If you drop your level, always recheck calibration before your next job.

Frequently asked questions

What length spirit level do I need for most home projects?

A 24-inch (600 mm) level is the most versatile for hanging shelves, leveling kitchen cabinets, and checking windows. For larger tasks like installing a deck or leveling a floor, a 48-inch (1200 mm) level is better. A 9-inch torpedo level is handy for tight spaces like inside electrical boxes or around baseboards.

What's the difference between a box level and an I-beam level?

Box levels have a rectangular cross-section (like a closed box) making them heavier and less likely to flex, so they’re preferred for rough construction and masonry work. I-beam levels have an open ‘I’ profile, making them lighter and easier to handle for finish work and hanging items; they are also less likely to rock on uneven surfaces.

Can I use a spirit level as a straightedge?

Many levels are designed to double as straightedges, especially box levels with a flat edge. However, I-beam levels may flex under pressure, so check the manufacturer’s spec. For critical straightedge work like checking a countertop or saw blade alignment, use a dedicated straightedge or a level with a machined edge rated for straightness.

How do I use a spirit level for plumb (vertical) checks?

Hold the level vertically against the surface you want to check, with the vial window facing you. The bubble should be centered between the lines on the plumb vial. Move the top or bottom of the level left or right until the bubble is centered. For a truly vertical surface, the level must also be level itself, rock it perpendicular to the wall until the horizontal vial is centered.

Are cheap spirit levels worth buying for occasional use?

A budget level can be fine for basic tasks like hanging a picture frame, but the vial accuracy may drift over time, and the frame may warp. For any job where being off by a quarter-inch matters (e.g., tiling, countertops), invest in a mid-range brand like Stabila, Empire, or Stanley. The difference in reliability is noticeable.

In shortSpirit levels are simple tools, but small differences in accuracy, length, and features can save you hours of frustration and rework. Focus on what fits your typical jobs: a general-purpose length with a good accuracy rating (0.5 mm/m or tighter) and a sturdy frame that won’t flex. Keep it clean, check it regularly, and store it safely, and it will serve you faithfully for years.
